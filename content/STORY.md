# The story bible

**Read this before writing a scene.** `characters.yaml` says who exists and
which voice they own; this says what they want and what is at stake. Without
it, scenes become a sequence of errands — grammatically correct, correctly
levelled, and impossible to care about. Nobody finishes a course they do not
care about, and a beginner who stops has learned nothing whatever the
validator said.

Four blocks, 24 units, A0 → A2. One continuous story.

---

## The premise

Miguel has just arrived in a new city. He can do the work, he is not stupid,
and he cannot say so. Everyone he meets forms an opinion of him in the four
seconds before he finds a word.

That gap — between who he is and how much of it survives translation — is the
story. It is also the learner's actual life, which is why the course has a
protagonist at all rather than a series of dialogues.

**Miguel wins.** Not instantly and not without setbacks, but the direction of
the whole arc is upward, and each block ends with him able to do something he
could not do at the start. A course for someone who already suspects he is bad
at English cannot tell a story where the immigrant struggles and stays stuck.

---

## What each block is about

The can-do statements come from `curriculum.yaml`. The stakes are here.

### Block 1 (A0) — *Primeros Sonidos* — **to be seen**

Miguel can say nothing, so the stake is the smallest one there is: being
treated as a person by someone who does not share his language. A greeting
returned is a win. The block ends with a neighbour who knows his name.

> Beat: he is a stranger → someone learns his name → he says something true.

### Block 2 (A1) — *Mi Día* — **to belong**

Miguel has work now, alongside Carlos. He can do the job fine. What he cannot
do is join the conversation at lunch, and he sits through it every day. The
stake is the difference between being employed somewhere and being *of* it.

The block ends with him telling Ana about his family without anyone helping.

> Beat: he is competent and silent → he says one thing wrong and survives it →
> he tells someone something that matters.

### Block 3 (A1+) — *En la Calle* — **to manage alone**

Carlos has been translating for him. This block is Miguel doing things without
Carlos: a purchase, a bus, directions to somewhere he has never been.

**This is the block with the real setback.** He gets something wrong on his own
— the wrong bus, the wrong order, an appointment he misses — and the point of
the block is that *he fixes it himself*. Not Carlos, not Ana. Him, with the
English he has, which is not enough and works anyway.

> Beat: he goes out alone → it goes wrong → he repairs it.

### Block 4 (A2) — *Ayer y Mañana* — **to have a voice**

Rosa, his supervisor. Something happened at work and Miguel has to explain it:
what happened, why, and what he is going to do. This is the whole course
cashing out — past tense, future tense, and a problem that needs both.

Rosa is direct and never unkind, and she listens. The block ends with Miguel
being understood about something that costs him if he is not.

> Beat: something goes wrong → he explains it in the past tense → he is
> believed.

## The cast, deeper

`characters.yaml` is authoritative for id, name, and voice. Never add a
character: a seventh person needs a seventh permanent voice, and the recognition
that makes the cast work is exactly what a stranger dilutes.

**Miguel** — the learner's counterpart, explicitly *"igual que tú"*. He is
allowed to hesitate, to ask for repetition, and to be wrong on the audio. He
should never be the joke. His competence is established early and outside
English: he is good at his work, and the language is the only thing in his way.

**Ana** — the neighbour, and the story's warmth. She repeats things without
being asked and without making a performance of it. She is the person who
treats Miguel as an adult before he can prove he is one. She is not his
teacher and never explains grammar.

**Carlos** — ten years in, switches between both languages. He is Miguel's
shortcut and therefore his obstacle: every time Carlos translates, Miguel does
not have to try. Block 3 exists because Carlos is not there.

**Maria** — runs the café. Fast and warm, which makes her the honest listening
challenge: she is not slowing down for anybody, and she likes him anyway. She
is where the learner meets real speed.

**Rosa** — the supervisor. Direct, clear, never cruel. She matters enormously
because she is the authority figure, and a nervous beginner expects authority
to be a threat. Rosa is proof it is not. **She never punishes Miguel for his
English** — if she corrects anything, it is a fact, not his grammar.

**Tom** — English, and his accent is the lesson. He exists so the learner does
not overfit to American vowels. He is a friend, not a difficulty.

## Rules for writing scenes

1. **Something is at stake in every scene, even at A0.** "Miguel greets Ana" is
   not a scene. "Miguel greets Ana and she stops to talk" is.
2. **Miguel talks.** He is the protagonist and the learner's proxy; a scene
   where he is discussed and silent teaches the wrong lesson twice.
3. **No scene punishes him for his English.** People can misunderstand him.
   Nobody sneers.
4. **Keep the world small.** The apartment, the café, the workplace, the bus,
   one clinic, one shop. A small world recurs, and recurrence is what makes
   `Maria` mean something by unit 12.
5. **Immigration, police, employers, clinics: careful.** These are real fears
   for this learner. They may appear — pretending they do not exist is its own
   condescension — but Miguel is never in legal jeopardy, never asked for
   papers, and never afraid of the person in front of him. The tension is
   always linguistic, never existential.
6. **Nobody explains English inside a scene.** No character is a teacher. The
   course teaches; the story is just people talking.
7. **Six scenes per unit are one afternoon,** not six unrelated encounters.

## Block 1, unit by unit

Unit 1 is authored. The other five are the next thing to write; the vocabulary
each may use is in `content/vocab-schedule.yaml` and is currently empty for all
of them, which is the other half of this job.

| Unit | Can-do | Beat |
|---|---|---|
| **b1_u1** *(done)* | Greet, give a name | Miguel arrives. Ana, the café, Carlos. He learns that a greeting returned costs nothing. |
| **b1_u2** | Numbers, age, yes/no | A form he has to fill in — a phone number, an age. A small bureaucratic moment he survives without help. |
| **b1_u3** | Family and people | Ana asks who is in the photo. He misses them, and saying so is the first true thing he manages in English. |
| **b1_u4** | Places, where things are | He gets lost on the way to the café. Someone points. He arrives. |
| **b1_u5** | Time, days, routine | Rosa gives him his hours. He has to read them back and gets one wrong. |
| **b1_u6** | Wants and needs | He orders at Maria's counter, alone, and gets what he asked for. The block ends here on purpose. |

Unit 6 is the payoff for the whole block: the first thing Miguel does entirely
alone, in a place the learner has watched him be silent in since unit 1.
